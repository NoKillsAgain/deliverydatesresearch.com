West Texas is close. The numbers are not the same.

Originally published Nov 12, 2026 on Substack.

One firm puts the region's third-party inventory almost even with Northern Virginia. Another puts existing inventory lower. The dates that govern both markets, and the towns around them, are local.

Delivery Dates Research reads the dates companies put in their SEC filings, then checks later filings to see what happened. This week we also read the markets around those filings: where the largest campuses are being built, what now sits in front of a delivery date, and whether the same constraints are showing up in housing and public construction.

Avison Young, citing datacenterHawk in its third-quarter 2026 U.S. overview, puts West Texas third-party inventory at 6,789 megawatts, just behind Northern Virginia at 6,884 megawatts. The chart footnote is specific: “Includes third-party-built facilities that have delivered or have a committed tenant.” That count is not limited to capacity already operating.

JLL’s Midyear 2026 report, issued with an August 11, 2026 newsroom release, is a different measurement. On a map labeled “Market sizing (existing MW),” West Texas is marked 733 megawatts. The same report says “West Texas has been the largest beneficiary of this industry shift.” The newsroom release says “Texas has cemented its position as the state for data centers, with 26 GW of existing and under-construction capacity,” and that “frontier markets now account for 77% of all capacity under development.”

Those figures can sit in the same story. They cannot be added, averaged, or treated as one operating total. The 733-megawatt figure is existing inventory, midyear. The 6,789-megawatt figure is third-party inventory in the third quarter, including facilities that have a committed tenant even if they have not yet delivered. We are not publishing a single “West Texas is X megawatts” number.

Construction has moved toward power-rich frontier markets, and West Texas is at the center of that move. For the companies we track, the useful question is not which brochure wins. It is which calendar actually governs a delivery date, and whether that calendar is reaching the rest of the community.

Northern Virginia: a pause that has not been adopted

In Loudoun County, the pause that has been widely described as a fact is still a proposal.

On September 15, 2026, the Board of Supervisors voted to have staff prepare a resolution that, if approved, would pause the Board’s final action on legislative data center and substation applications for up to 12 months. The official county news release two days later, on September 17, was careful about the tense: “the majority of the Board voted in favor of future consideration of a resolution that, if approved, would pause the Board’s action.” It also noted “a legal opinion from the Loudoun County Attorney indicating that a moratorium on data center approvals is not legal.”

That resolution was not adopted on October 20. A county news release that day said the item “will be deferred to the Board’s November 17 meeting” so staff can “develop more information that will help inform the Board’s ultimate decision.” As of November 12, 2026, there is no adopted pause and no effective date in force. The next scheduled vote is November 17.

Prince William County already made a change. On September 22, 2026, the Board of County Supervisors adopted ordinance No. 26-67, restructuring the Data Center Opportunity Zone Overlay District. The county planning office says the overlay “is closed to any future expansion.” Parcels that remain inside it and meet the new eligibility rules may still develop by right. Outside the overlay, new data center proposals “must now proceed through the Special Use Permit (SUP) process.” A final eligibility period for remaining in the amended overlay “closes on December 21, 2026,” after which the county says it will publish an updated boundary map.

Texas: December 10

In Texas, a different date now sits in front of large new loads.

Avison Young’s third-quarter report, citing the governor’s office, the Public Utility Commission of Texas, and ERCOT, lists a 2026 sequence of large-load rules and then this line: “Coming December 10: ERCOT is expected to file its queue audit and community-impact reports with PUCT.” The same timeline says new interconnection rules took effect October 8 “for loads greater than 75 MW.”

The pause on turning those loads on is stated in ERCOT’s Batch Zero Update for its September 14-15, 2026 board meeting: “Paused approvals to energize data centers or virtual currency mining facilities (crypto facilities) that are 75 MW or greater.” The same update says ERCOT will file its Batch Zero Eligibility Verification Report and its Community Impact Review Report “with the Commission by December 10, 2026.”

December 10 is not a company’s delivery date. It is a filing date for the grid operator, and the checkpoint now sitting in front of those paused approvals. It still belongs under every Texas delivery promise we track.

Abilene: houses take longer, and permits are up

The clearest on-the-record delay we found in these communities is housing in Abilene.

Gene Lantrip, an Abilene homebuilder, told the Texas Tribune on April 28, 2026: “It’s taken us two months longer to build the houses than what it did before the data centers were coming in.” He added, “That’s the downside.” In the same story he said his electrician “lost two of his lead men and several of his helpers to the data center.”

The city’s own planning report does not describe a permit freeze. Timothy A. Littlejohn, director of Planning and Development Services, wrote in the third-quarter 2026 report that “the construction activity related to data centers has created positive results seen in Abilene’s economy, however, we are still in need of affordable housing in an inflated market.” Building inspections issued 280 permits for new single-family residences in the quarter, compared with 194 in the same quarter a year earlier. Total residential permits were 324, up from 281.

Littlejohn’s report separately describes a housing crunch tied to Dyess. He wrote: “Adding to the heat’s sizzle is the ongoing housing crunch. Dyess Air Force Base is preparing for the arrival of the B-21 Raider and associated newcomers involved with these projects. We currently do not have enough housing available.” The Tribune likewise ties tighter housing supply to “data center workers and an upcoming expanded mission at Dyess Air Force Base.”

Homebuilders say houses now take longer, and they point to electricians leaving for data center work. Single-family permitting is up. The city’s letter ties the housing crunch to the coming Dyess mission and describes data-center construction as having created “positive results seen in Abilene’s economy.”

Phoenix: the wait is often a transformer

In Phoenix, the constraint that shows up most clearly is equipment, and it is not limited to data centers.

AZ Big Media, on November 11, 2026, quoting LGE Design Build’s third-quarter Construction Delivery Outlook, reported: “Medium-voltage switchgear lead times of 52 to 80 weeks are concentrated in the Phoenix market, while pad-mount transformers for standard commercial and industrial service run 40 to 65 weeks. Hyperscale and utility-scale projects face substation transformer lead times of 90 to 160 weeks, so a project that clears every regulatory hurdle may still wait two to three years to be energized.”

Blake Wells, LGE’s vice president of preconstruction, said: “Demand in Phoenix is still strong across major sectors, but the variables that determine whether a project gets built on schedule have changed.” The same outlook says access to power “has become the deciding factor in whether Phoenix construction projects move forward on schedule, even as demand remains healthy across industrial, office and retail.”

Georgia and Ohio: fencing the bill

Regulators in Georgia and Ohio have tried to let large loads connect while keeping the cost off residential bills.

The Georgia Public Service Commission’s September 2026 data center fact sheet records a July 31, 2025 order of “a freeze of Georgia Power base rates through 2028.” In the next sentence the Commission wrote that, “while the Commission continues its mission to prevent new data centers from shifting costs to residential customers, rates cannot increase.” The same fact sheet says the Commission “continues to ensure current ratepayers will not subsidize data centers.” Under the August 26, 2026 bullet, it also records a letter in which Georgia Power agreed: “If a data center ends a contract early, Georgia Power will not seek to recover from residential and small business customers any incremental cost associated with the data center during the unfulfilled term of the contract.”

In Ohio, the Public Utilities Commission took up AEP Ohio’s moratorium on new data center connections and, on July 9, 2025, ordered the company to replace it with a data-center-specific tariff and “lift its moratorium on connecting new data centers as soon as possible.” PUCO Chair Jenifer French said the order “safeguards non-data center customers on an industrial and residential level while establishing a dependable and reasonable environment for data centers to continue to thrive within Ohio.”

Those are rate-design decisions, not delivery dates. A market that fences costs and lifts a connection freeze is trying to keep projects moving without putting the bill on households.

What the filings we track now say

Some of the dates that will test these calendars are already in SEC filings.

IREN’s Form 10-Q for the quarter ended September 30, 2026, filed November 5, says: “Horizon 1 was delivered to and accepted by Microsoft in August 2026 and remains in service.” The same 10-Q later says, “certain contracted capacity that we had targeted to deliver by December 31, 2026 is now expected to be delivered during the following quarter, as a result of a customer-requested design change, supply timing and third-party delays, and there can be no assurance that this capacity will be completed on the revised timeline.” The 10-Q does not name Horizons 2-4 in that moved-date sentence. We are not counting this as a Horizons 2-4 slip until a filing ties it to them.

Riot Platforms’ Form 8-K, dated October 29, 2026 and filed November 4, Exhibit 99.1, gives this Corsicana delivery schedule: “400 MW in September 2027, 200 MW in January 2028 and 310 MW in July 2028, with rent on each phase commencing no later than 60 days after delivery of its powered pads.” Those are three dates we will check against later filings. They are dates for powered pads, not for a tenant’s computers coming on.

Galaxy Digital, in a July 6, 2026 announcement about Phase I at its Helios campus in West Texas, wrote: “Phase I was delivered on schedule, with rent commencement under the Phase I lease beginning in the second quarter of 2026.” Phase II “data hall deliveries expected to commence in the first half of 2027” is the next Helios date on the page.

What we did not find

We looked again for a primary local document, such as school-board minutes, a hospital board packet, or a municipal staff memo, that names a specific school or hospital delayed because a data center took its power connection, labor, or equipment. We did not find one.

A delivery date in a filing is a company’s statement about its own work. The calendars around it, at the county board, at the grid operator, and in the electrician’s shop, now have dates of their own. We will read the next filings against both.
