Leased Is Not Billing

Originally published Nov 13, 2026 on Substack.

Two builders reported on November 5 and 6. Both counted capacity in stages. The gap between the stages is where a delivery date lives.

Two builders reported on November 5 and 6. Both counted capacity in stages. The gap between the stages is where a delivery date lives.

Core Scientific’s third-quarter results release has a page most readers will skip. It is a glossary. The company defines five ways to count the same megawatts, and two of them sit next to each other.

“Leased Customer Power Capacity” is “Power capacity that is committed to customers under executed customer contracts, regardless of whether service has commenced as of period end.” “Billable Customer Power Capacity” is the “Portion of Leased Customer Power Capacity for which service has commenced, and we are actively billing as of period end.”

A signed contract on one line. A working hall on the next. Everything a delivery date promises happens between them.

Core Scientific: 485 of 590

The quarterly report, filed November 5, puts the two numbers side by side for the company’s first customer. “Billable customer power capacity of 485 MW as of September 30, 2026, against 590 MW of leased customer power capacity to CoreWeave, with the remaining 105 MW in various stages of construction and commissioning.”

The billing count has climbed each quarter. The company’s results tables list 225 billing megawatts for the first quarter, 395 for the second and 485 for the third. Its chief executive, Adam Sullivan, said: “We are nearing full delivery under our first colocation agreement while construction under our second is underway across five campuses.”

The second agreement is younger, and its date is a year, not a quarter. In July the company signed leases with AMD and a counterparty it calls a Neocloud, “for an aggregate of approximately 530 MW of critical IT capacity across five of our sites.” The quarterly report says: “As contracted capacity is constructed and delivered across five sites, revenue under these agreements is expected to commence in phases beginning in 2027.”

The same report names what would follow a miss. “To the extent we are unable to deliver contracted capacity on schedule, or if our customer relationships were disrupted, our revenue recognition, deferred revenue obligations and capital recovery could be materially affected.”

TeraWulf: three stages, three dates

TeraWulf reported a day later and counted in the same stages, with different words. At its Lake Mariner campus in New York, the company “Operated 102 MW of revenue-generating critical IT capacity as of September 30, 2026, with an additional 336 MW under construction across CB-4 and CB-5.”

Each building has its own date. “CB-4 remains on track for phased delivery and rent commencement during the fourth quarter. CB-5 remains targeted to begin phased delivery in early 2027.”

The August release gave CB-4 a wider window. Then, “The first CB-4 data hall has entered commissioning, with phased delivery and rent commencement expected during the second half of 2026, while CB-5 remains targeted to begin phased delivery in early 2027.” The second half of the year has since narrowed to its last quarter, which began October 1. The CB-5 sentence did not change.

Kentucky: the words changed, too

The longest date in the release belongs to the Justified campus in Hawesville, Kentucky, where TeraWulf signed a 20-year lease with Anthropic. In August, the company wrote of that lease: “initial delivery expected in the second half of 2027 and full delivery expected in early 2028.”

The November release says something close, in different terms. “Phased ready-for-service completions are expected to begin in late 2027 and continue through the second quarter of 2028.” The same words appear in the November 4 announcement of the construction contract with Fluor, which says the agreement “establishes defined delivery milestones, open-book cost reporting, procurement oversight and contractual performance protections.”

Two things are different. The word “delivery” became “ready-for-service,” and “early 2028” became “through the second quarter of 2028.” The filings do not say whether these describe the same finish line. We do not count it as a moved date. We record both sentences and will read the next one against them.

The last date is the furthest out, and it comes with a condition. TeraWulf raised its contracted power at the Muskie campus in eastern Kentucky to 1,000 MW, “subject to approval by the Kentucky Public Service Commission.” The release says the expansion “advances the anticipated delivery of the second 500 MW phase to 2029.”

What the stages tell a reader

Set the two reports side by side and the same campus can be described four ways: power under contract, capacity leased to a customer, capacity under construction, and capacity a customer is paying for. Each count measures something real. Only the last is a hall in service.

That is why we read the stage, not just the date. A promise that capacity will be “leased” is met on the day a contract is signed. A promise that it will be “billing” is met only when the building is finished, commissioned and handed over. The dates in these filings attach to the second kind.

Our ledger records how often dates of that kind hold. Of 79 dated promises we have checked, 29 were not delivered on time: 21 arrived late and 8 were moved. When a date moves, the move is information, not wrongdoing. The CB-4 window closes at the end of December, and the next quarterly reports will show where the 105 MW stands.
