The Equipment Calendar

Originally published Nov 10, 2026 on Substack.

Two AI data-center builders reported five days apart. Each moved a date. Each pointed, at least in part, to its suppliers.

On November 5, IREN told investors that the target at the center of its AI cloud plan had moved. The figure, its earnings release said, was "previously year-end 2026, is now targeted for January 2027 following a customer-requested change and supplier timing, and may extend to February 2027".

Five days later, on November 10, Bitdeer filed its third-quarter release. Its lead line was reassurance: "Tydal remains on target". Further down, in the site table, a different project carried a different note. For Knoxville, Tennessee, the company wrote: "Due to delivery delays for critical power and electrical equipment, energization is now targeted for Q4 2027".

Neither change should be read as anyone doing anything wrong. A moved date is not misconduct. It is information, and it is worth reading closely.

What changed, in their words

IREN's co-CEO, Daniel Roberts, put the move in one clause in the same release: the target is "now targeted for January, after a customer-requested design change and supplier timing moved it from year-end".

Two causes, then. One comes from the customer. The other comes from the supply chain. The release also says what happens after a site is handed over. Revenue, it says, is "expected to ramp upon, and being subject to, commissioning, testing and customer acceptance in the months following each data center's delivery." A building can be delivered before it earns. The calendar has more than one finish line.

Bitdeer's Knoxville entry has a history in its own filings. In its second-quarter release, filed August 10, the company wrote: "To meet customer demand for larger scale deployments, we have fully redesigned the project, with overall completion now targeted for Q3 ’27." Three months later, the date in the table reads Q4'27, and the stated cause is no longer design. It is equipment.

The Tydal project in Norway, the one the release headlined, held its dates. Phase 1 is listed for Q4'26 and phase 2 for Q1'27, the same quarters shown in August.

The part that rarely makes a headline

Read together, the two filings point at the same pressure. It is not land, and in these two sentences it is not money. It is "critical power and electrical equipment" in one case and "supplier timing" in the other.

IREN's own risk language names it plainly. Among its listed risks: "delays, increases in costs or reductions in the supply of materials or equipment used in our development or operations". This quarter, a schedule moved and the company pointed to supplier timing.

Bitdeer's footnote to its backlog makes the same link from the other side. The timing of future revenue, it says, depends "on, among other things, data center energization, GPU deployment and customer performance."

Energization is the word to watch. It is the moment a site draws power. Everything after it, the racks, the testing, the customer sign-off, waits on that day.

How this fits the record

At Delivery Dates Research we track dated promises in public filings and check them against what the companies later said. In our set of 79 dated promises from bitcoin miners and AI data-center builders, 29 slipped: 21 were delivered late and 8 were moved to a later date. The late deliveries came in a median of 62 days late.

Neither November change is in that 79-row set, which was locked on October 30. If they were, both would count as moved dates, not late deliveries. Each company moved its own date in a filing, and each gave a reason. That is the record working as it should. The question for the next quarter is narrower: whether January holds for IREN, and whether Q4'27 holds for Knoxville, and what each company says if it does not.

We will read those filings when they arrive.
