The date before the date: why grid approvals are the earliest public clue to a delivery slip

Originally published Oct 29, 2026 on Substack.

By K. Foster, DD Research

Before a large data center can draw power, a lot has to happen. The building has to go up, the equipment has to arrive, and the utility has to be ready. In California there’s a step before all of that, and it’s public. The utility has to get the state regulator to sign off on the agreement that connects the customer to the grid.

That sign-off has a date. The date is written down months or years before anyone talks about when the lights come on. When it moves, everything stacked on top of it is being asked to move too.

A live example came up this week, and credit goes to California Regulatory Intelligence (@calregulatory), whose Thursday briefing laid it out.

The case: PG&E and Google in San Jose

On December 18, 2025, PG&E filed Advice Letter 7785-E with the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC). The letter asked the CPUC to approve a transmission interconnection agreement for a Google data center in San Jose. It was filed as an exceptional case under PG&E’s Electric Rules 2, 15 and 16. According to the revised filing, the site is at 5079 Disk Drive and the load is 250 MW at full build.

The original letter set out a chain of dates. It asked for approval by June 1, 2026, so that construction could begin in January 2027 and the facilities could be operational by December 2028. The CPUC’s own draft resolution restates that chain: approval by June 1, 2026, construction beginning in January 2027, and “an operational in-service date in December 2028.”

That’s three dates, each depending on the one before it, and the first is a regulatory date.

Step by step

1. Filing (Dec 18, 2025). PG&E files a Tier 3 advice letter. A Tier 3 letter needs a formal Commission resolution, not staff sign-off.

2. Protests (January 2026). According to the draft resolution, Imel Enterprises protested on January 6 and the CPUC’s Public Advocates Office protested on January 7. PG&E responded on January 14.

3. Draft resolution (May 29, 2026). According to the revised filing, the CPUC’s Energy Division issued Draft Resolution E-5455 on May 29, and parties filed comments on June 18. A draft is a proposal, not an approval. The requested June 1 approval date passed with the item still in draft.

4. Revised draft (Aug 13, 2026). The revised draft proposed approving the agreement with modifications. One of them was an added capital advance of $600,000 per MW, which comes to $150 million for 250 MW.

5. Revised agreement (Oct 26, 2026). PG&E filed AL 7785-E-A, a supplement that replaces the original letter. It adds the advance and other terms responding to the draft. A supplemental filing opens a new protest window, and this one runs to November 16, 2026.

6. Vote. E-5455 is item 2 on the agenda for the CPUC’s October 29, 2026 voting meeting. The CPUC’s hold list for that meeting shows item 2 held to November 19, 2026, by staff. calregulatory’s briefing describes this as the item being postponed “once again.”

7. Construction and service. These are the stated targets of January 2027 and December 2028. The draft resolution repeated them, and no public filing since has restated them. The agreement’s Exhibit A carries the “Estimated In-Service Date,” but it sits in a confidential attachment, so the public record doesn’t show whether the December 2028 target changed in the revision.

Why this is the earliest clue

Delivery dates usually reach the public late. They show up in a quarterly report, an earnings call or a press release, often after the schedule has already shifted. The approval date is different. It’s in the first filing, it’s specific, and anyone can see whether it was met by reading a regulator’s public agenda.

In this case, the requested approval date was June 1, 2026. As of today the item is held to November 19, more than five months later, with a protest window still open. That doesn’t mean construction will start late. Schedules can absorb some slack, and the parties may have planned for it. It does mean the first date in the chain has moved, and the dates after it now depend on how much slack there was.

None of this points to anyone doing anything wrong. Revisions, protests and holds are how the process is supposed to work: a regulator asks questions and the parties answer them. The point is narrower. The regulatory step is where a timeline first becomes testable in public.

Not a one-off

The revised filing lists earlier agreements of the same type that the CPUC approved. Their own advice letters show how the approval step went:

  • Microsoft, San Jose (AL 7635-E, 90 MW): filed July 1, 2025, with a decision requested by November 30, 2025, and a target of operation in January 2027. It was approved by Resolution E-5439 on January 15, 2026.

  • Menlo Equities, Sunnyvale (AL 7667-E, 49 MW): filed August 4, 2025, with approval requested by December 31, 2025, construction by July 1, 2026, and operation by June 2027. It was approved by Resolution E-5433 on March 19, 2026.

  • Equinix and RiCloud, San Jose (AL 5848-E): filed June 18, 2020, for a substation expected to be operational in April 2021. It was approved by Resolution E-5121 on May 6, 2021.

In each case, the letter’s own words give the requested date, and PG&E’s list in the revised filing gives the date of the resolution. Putting them side by side shows how far the approval moved.

What we’re doing with it

We’re adding utility-commission approval dates to the dates we track, starting with these CPUC filings. For each agreement we log the requested approval date, the draft resolution dates, the vote and the stated construction and in-service dates. When a later filing restates a date, we put the new date next to the original and keep both.

For San Jose, the next dates to watch are November 16, when protests close, and November 19, when the item is next scheduled.
