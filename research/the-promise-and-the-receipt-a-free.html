The Promise and the Receipt: A Free Course on Checking Delivery Dates

Originally published Oct 28, 2026 on Substack.

Five short chapters, one free browser, zero paid tools. How to check a company’s promised date against what its SEC filings later prove.

By K. Foster, DD Research

You’ve lived this already. The pizza tracker says “out for delivery” for forty minutes. The contractor says the kitchen will be “basically done by spring.” The gate agent says “just a short delay.” Nobody’s lying, exactly. But the only thing that tells you what really happened is the receipt.

Companies are the same. A press release is the promise, and an SEC filing is the receipt. This course shows you how to put the two side by side yourself, for free, on SEC EDGAR.

The whole course in five lines:

  1. Circle the verb. “Begin,” “energize” and “complete” are three different promises.

  2. Find the filing that proves what happened: a 10-Q, a 10-K, or an 8-K and its Exhibit 99.1.

  3. Count with two rules: starting isn’t finishing, and the calendar doesn’t get credit.

  4. Practice on a made-up example until the rules feel automatic.

  5. Log every check in one table, with two free links per row.

Chapter 1: The verb is the promise

Here’s a typical line: “We expect to energize the site in Q2.” Before you even look at the date, circle the verb.

  • Begin, commence, break ground: something will start.

  • Energize, bring online, commission: something will work.

  • Complete, deliver, finish: something will be done.

“I’ll start the diet Monday” and “I’ll fit into the suit Monday” are not the same promise, and neither are “begin construction by June” and “complete construction by June.”

Then turn the deadline into a calendar date, using the latest day the wording allows. “Q2” means June 30, not April 1. “Mid-year,” “by year-end” and “in the coming months” each set different deadlines, so write down exactly what was said.

Try it: Open a company’s latest press release on its investor-relations page. Copy the promise sentence word for word, underline the verb, and write the deadline as a date. Keep the release URL and its publication date.

Chapter 2: Go find the receipt

Press releases describe what a company hopes will happen. Filings report what did happen, and anyone can read them free on EDGAR.

  • 10-Q and 10-K: read the MD&A (Management’s Discussion and Analysis) and any “business update” or “recent developments” section. Dates often get quietly updated here.

  • 8-K, Item 7.01 or Item 8.01: these are updates between quarters.

  • Exhibit 99.1: often the press release or monthly update itself, attached to an 8-K. Once it’s an exhibit, it’s on the record with a filing date.

Search tips for EDGAR full-text search (https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/):

  • Put exact phrases in quotes, like "energized" or "commenced construction".

  • Filter by company name or CIK, then by form type.

  • Set the date range from the promise date to today.

  • Search the project or site name too. Companies rename sites less often than they reschedule them.

Think of the filing as the fitness tracker after “I totally went to the gym.” It doesn’t care about vibes. It has a timestamp.

Try it: Run one search for your project name, filtered to the company and to dates after the promise. Open the earliest filing that mentions it and copy the status sentence word for word.

Chapter 3: Two rules that keep everyone honest

Without rules, the same project can look on time or slipped depending on who’s squinting. These two remove the squinting.

Rule A: Starting isn’t finishing. Match the evidence to the verb. If the promise was to begin, proof that work started counts as on time. If the promise was to finish or energize, breaking ground doesn’t count. “Your table will be ready at 8” is met when you sit down, not when someone starts wiping the table.

Rule B: The calendar doesn’t get credit. The proven date is the exact date the filing gives for delivery, even if it falls in the middle of a month. If the filing gives no exact date, the proven date is the filing date. So if a June monthly report filed on July 9 says only that the site was “energized during June,” the proven date is July 9, not June 30. The work may well have happened in June, but the record only proves it on July 9, and we count the record.

The outcomes:

  • Delivered on time: proven on or before the deadline.

  • Slipped: proven after the deadline, or the deadline passed and the filing still says it hasn’t happened.

  • Slipped (moved): a filing sets a new, later date. That counts as slipped. Record it and keep tracking until a filing proves delivery.

One more thing. A slip is a fact about a date, not a judgment about anyone. Projects run late for a hundred ordinary reasons. Ask anyone whose flight was “delayed 20 minutes” four times in a row.

Chapter 4: A worked example (HYPOTHETICAL)

Everything here is invented. The company, the site and the dates are fictional and don’t describe any real business.

On January 15, Lumen Ridge Compute Co. (fictional) says: “We expect to energize our Cedar Flats facility in Q2.”

  • The promise: the verb is energize, and the deadline is June 30.

  • April filing: an 8-K Item 7.01 with Exhibit 99.1 says “construction commenced at Cedar Flats.”

  • July 9 filing: an 8-K Item 8.01 attaches the June monthly update, which says “Cedar Flats was energized during June.”

Rule A: “Construction commenced” is a start, and the promise was to energize, so April doesn’t count. Nice shovel, though. Rule B: The first filing that proves energization is dated July 9.

Verdict: Slipped by 9 days. That’s a fact about a date, not a scandal. The lesson is the method: a report that “it happened in June,” filed in July, proves July.

Now flip it. If the April filing had said “Cedar Flats was energized on April 28,” the proven date would be April 28, and the verdict would be Delivered on time, like a pizza that shows up before the oven timer beeps.

Try it: Re-run the example on paper, then change one date and see whether the verdict flips.

Chapter 5: Keep a log, not a hunch

One check is trivia. Fifty checks in the same format are a record. Use one row per promise:

House rules:

  • Copy wording word for word. Paraphrasing is how “begin” turns into “finish.”

  • Every row needs two free links, one to the promise and one to the proof.

  • Rows that are still waiting stay open until a filing proves them or the deadline passes.

  • When a date moves, add a new row and link it to the old one. Don’t overwrite history.
