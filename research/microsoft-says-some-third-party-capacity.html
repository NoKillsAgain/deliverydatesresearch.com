Microsoft Says Some Third-Party Capacity Arrived Late

Originally published Oct 30, 2026 on Substack.

What a delivery date looks like from both sides of the contract

By K. Foster, DD Research

On Oct 28, during Microsoft’s quarterly earnings call, CFO Amy Hood said:

“Some third-party capacity deliveries we expected in Q1 were delayed.”

She added that Microsoft now expects capital spending to pick up “in the second half of the fiscal year,” driven in part by “the 3P capacity deliveries that shifted to H2.” (CNBC, Oct 28)

Microsoft’s fiscal year starts in July. Its fiscal Q1 ran from July through September, and the second half of its fiscal year runs from January through June 2027. A day later, CNBC’s follow-up put it in calendar terms: the delays “will push out some available capacity until the first half of 2027.” According to the Oct 28 report, Hood tied the delays to GPU-as-a-service providers.

No supplier is named, and that isn’t the interesting part anyway. What matters is that this is a delivery date seen from the customer’s side. Most of the time, we only get to see the builder’s side.

Two sides, one date

Every capacity contract has a date in it, and both parties talk about that date in public, each in its own language.

The customer talks about it as timing. A quarter’s capacity “shifted,” spending moves “to H2,” and the date shows up as a change in a spending plan.

The builder talks about it as a schedule. Halls are “expected to be delivered” in a window, rent “will commence” at delivery, and a phase reaches “Ready for Service.”

Usually you only hear one side. This week Microsoft spoke first, which makes it a good time to look at how builders phrase the same kind of promise.

How builders state a date: two recent examples

These two companies are examples of how builders write dates. Nothing in their filings that we read says either one is part of Microsoft’s delay.

1. A window that moved, stated in an 8-K. In an 8-K filed Sept 25, Cipher disclosed a Sept 24 amendment to its Barber Lake lease with Fluidstack. Alongside design change orders, the parties agreed to:

“an updated phased delivery schedule for the Barber Lake Facility, with individual data halls now expected to be delivered between the fourth quarter of 2026 and the first quarter of 2027.”

The same filing says “Rent will commence for each data hall as it is delivered.” There are three things to notice:

  • The date is a window, not a day.

  • The window is phased, so it applies hall by hall, not to the whole site at once.

  • Money is tied to delivery, because rent starts as each hall arrives.

That last point is why delivery dates matter to anyone reading a filing. The date isn’t a construction detail. It’s when the contract starts paying.

2. A milestone that arrived, stated in a release. On Oct 2, Applied Digital announced that it had:

“achieved Ready for Service for the second phase of Building 2 at its Polaris Forge 1 campus, delivering an additional 75 MW of critical IT load across three 25 MW data halls.”

This is the other end of the life of a date: the moment it’s met. “Ready for Service” is a specific milestone term. It’s not “energized,” and it’s not “operational.” When you track dates, the exact word matters as much as the day, because a later filing can quietly swap one milestone word for another.

Reading a date from both sides

Here is the habit we’d suggest, whether you follow builders, tenants or both.

  1. Write down the first promise. Record the last day its wording allows (that's the due date), the milestone word, and where it appeared (8-K, 10-Q, release, call).

  2. Watch for the restatement. Builders restate dates in later filings. Customers restate them as timing, like “shifted to H2.” Either one counts as a data point.

  3. Translate the calendars. Microsoft’s “second half of the fiscal year” and CNBC’s “first half of 2027” describe the same months. Mixing up fiscal and calendar time is the easiest way to invent a slip that never happened, or to miss one that did.

  4. Keep the words with the dates. “Expected to be delivered,” “Ready for Service,” “energized,” and “commissioned” are not the same thing.

  5. Don’t read intent into a slip. Schedules move because of equipment, power, design changes and weather. A moved date is information, not an accusation.

Why the customer’s view matters

A builder’s filing tells you what was promised and, later, what was delivered. A customer’s comment tells you something filings often can’t: that capacity someone was counting on didn’t arrive when expected, and how far the plan moved. Hood’s remark is a case where the demand side put a time shift on the record in its own words.

When both sides talk about the same kind of date, you can check one against the other. That cross-check is the core of what we do: we log promised dates from public filings, follow them through later filings, and record when and how they change.
